THE SHORT VERSION

The specification already states your boundary, in its own words, at §1. It is not "dynamically stateful" — it
is **"the rules that govern state, never the state itself"**. Under that principle the ball belongs in the
resolved game because *the rules key on it*, not because its state changes; and its position stays out because
the state is exactly what is excluded. Your `notIn: ["ball"]` ruling turns out to be the boundary applied
correctly rather than an exception to it.

The slippery slope does not follow, and the corpus already proves it rather than my arguing it.

**But I have to correct myself on its standing, and the correction matters.** That sentence appears once, carries

Your recorded boundary statement is a different sentence, at §1 just above it, and it is explicitly labelled:

    "The boundary, in Christian's words (18 September): the authoritative game contains supported resolved

That is about support and closure. **It says nothing about the sport, and nothing about scope in the sense you are

**Why it is not quite your (b), and the gap is the point.** (b) describes an activity "operating within an

That single omission explains everything else in this audit. It is why RC-19 could carry the environment
informally for months, why removing RC-19 broke something nobody expected, and why your question had to be asked
at all. **The spec is not wrong about its own scope — it is silent about what lies on the other side of it.**


3 · DYNAMIC STATE IS NOT THE DISTINCTION — and something better already is

Here is where I think your hypothesis needs correcting rather than confirming.

**"Represent it because its state and relationships change" argues from the excluded side of the boundary.** The
spec holds nothing about the state of a game in progress, and names "who has the ball now" as the example. If the
ball earned its place by being dynamically stateful, the same reasoning would admit the thing §7 was written to
keep out.

**The distinction the architecture actually holds is `initial value + the rule that governs it`, and PS1 is the
precedent — yours, from 2 October.** Its own note:

    "WHAT IT IS: a team-valued current state. Its INITIAL value is a governed realization choice over the
     established teams ... POSSESSION_CHANGE changes that state."

That is §1 applied exactly: the representation carries an initial value and the rule that changes it, and does
not carry the live state. Read that way, your `notIn: ["ball"]` ruling is not an exception to the boundary — **it
is the boundary.** The ball is present because rules key on it; its position is absent because position during
play is state.

And §1.9 already refuses any geometric use of a dynamic location, which is the same principle a third time.

So: the architecture supports your distinction, it has a precedent, and the operative words are better than the
ones in the hypothesis. I would adopt the spec's own formulation rather than "dynamically stateful".

5 · EXISTENCE VERSUS STATE

Existence can be sport-inherent. What the resolved game needs is not the ball's *state* but the ball as a
**carrier the rules can key on**. The two statements you asked me to separate hold, and the middle one should be
narrower than you wrote it: the resolved game carries the object because rules key on it; that its relationships
change is *why* rules key on it, not itself the licence.

Measured, and this is the part that already behaves correctly: when a ball exists it appears as `{kind: "ball"}`
with no position and creates no realization freedom. That is now an architectural consequence rather than a
happy accident — the applicability entry implements the boundary, and the boundary is stated in §1.


6 · THE SLIPPERY SLOPE DOES NOT FOLLOW, AND THE CORPUS PROVES IT

The test is **"do the rules key on it?"**, and it is already the test in use:

    boundaries, field dimensions    the SESSION envelope supplies them; no activity authors them
    out-of-play behaviour           constructed from the envelope; five contracts decline to author it
    start, score                    left to by-construction; nothing authors their existence
    number of players               the session
    possession change               AUTHORED by three independent contracts — the rules key on it
    the ball                        the carrier that the authored possession rule presupposes
    offside, ordinary legal actions nothing keys on them anywhere, and nothing establishes them

So authorizing a ball does **not** commit us to authoring the Laws of the Game. It commits us to authoring the
carriers of relationships our activities explicitly key on — which is one object today, and would be one more
only when an activity keys a rule on something new.

**The real asymmetry, and §6 says it is deliberate:** the session can supply a *dimension* but nothing can supply
an *object*. §5.4 assigns object existence to SELECTION and the session owns only four rows, and §6 states the
general rule — a source outside the representation that needs a structural property inside it enters as a
selection item under its own contract, *with no separate route*. So the asymmetry is the design, not a gap in it.
That is why the ball is the odd case: its prerequisite is the one kind of thing that has only the selection route,
and out-of-play's prerequisite happens to be a dimension, which the session already owns.


7 · THE A04 CLASSIFICATION

Of your five options: **failure to propagate an inherent sport invariant into the resolved representation**,
caused by **an overly narrow support model**. Both, and in that order.

But the sharpest statement is not on your list, and I think it is the true one: **this is PSD-03's deferred
ownership question becoming load-bearing for the first time.** Out-of-play restarts were its first instance and
cost nothing, because an unauthored restart is simply `NOT_AUTHORED` and no gate cares. The ball is its second
instance, and it costs a gate failure — because SD-44 turned "the sport supplies this" from a tolerated silence
into an unmet prerequisite. **The open item did not change; what changed is that something finally depends on it.**
That is also why it surfaced in my work rather than yours: enforcing your reachability ruling is what made the
deferral expensive.

Against the alternatives:
- **Not missing canonical knowledge.** "Soccer is played with a ball" was never meant to be activity knowledge,
  and five contracts' own notes show the corpus deliberately not authoring facts of that kind.
- **Not an incorrect reachability prerequisite.** A possession change genuinely presupposes a ball; SD-44 is
  right. I tested the alternative you implied — that the real prerequisite is PS1 rather than an object — and it
  does not rescue the check, for a reason worth reporting on its own, below.

**The second-order finding: the possession relation is established nowhere.** PS1 is `NOT_AUTHORED` with reason
"no coverage" in A04, in the control that *does* have a ball, and in the full corpus. **No contract declares or
claims PS1 at all.** So a ball restores the trigger's reachability but leaves the relation that trigger governs
unestablished — and nothing blocks on that, which is why my probe realized cleanly with acceptance 0/0/0.

That means **"A04 is realizable" and "possession is coherently represented" are different states**, and only the
first is one decision away. I would want you to know that before treating a ball as the finish line.


8 · WHAT §5.4 SHOULD MEAN — not changed, as you asked

Its Claim currently reads *"Every object referenced by a rule, transition or objective exists **and is positioned
inside `area`**"*. Under the ownership model above it should say that every such object **exists**, and that an
object **whose location is a feature of the layout** is positioned inside the area — leaving an object whose
location is state to carry none. The Owner column needs no change: selection still owns existence and kind.

One thing to check before any edit: the clause may be load-bearing for the envelope-fit and layout-feasibility
gates. I have not traced that, and I would rather say so than guess.
