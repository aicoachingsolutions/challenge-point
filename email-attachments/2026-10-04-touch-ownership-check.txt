The substance of this note is the ownership check on the qualifying interaction, because the answer is **yes,

The short version is more encouraging than I expected: **most of the structure already exists, and what is

(a) DOES AN EXISTING CANONICAL MECHANISM REPRESENT A PLAYER-BALL CONTACT?

**Partially — and the part that is owned is never the interaction itself.** Seven mechanisms each own a
different adjacent fact:

- **GP-006 / GP-007, "Establish / Recover Functional Object Control"** owns the *semantic concept* — a
  performer-task-object relationship that enables meaningful subsequent action. But it lives in the knowledge
  core as a **problem class**, not as anything representable. The three-layer rule keeps it there.
- **`possession.team`** owns the resulting **state**, and its own note excludes *"any invented mechanism for how
  possession is gained or lost"*. So the dependency runs the wrong way for us: possession cannot supply the
  touch.
- **`value.primaryEvent.kind`** owns the **consequence**. Its vocabulary is the RPC library's scoring events,
  whose canonical definitions genuinely are player-ball acts — one reads *"a pass is received and controlled by
  a designated target player"*. But the token is opaque: there is no actor field on the primary event.
- **`transitions[].qualifiers.lastTouch`** owns **team attribution** at an out-of-play trigger. Its register
  gloss is the only machine-readable contact statement in the whole register: *"whose touch last played the
  ball before it left the field."*
- **The restart actor and method** own the one represented performer-on-ball act — who restarts and by what
  delivery — but only at a stoppage, never in play.
- **`rules.actionRestrictions[]`** owns an authored **action's legal eligibility**, and its closed kind list is
  pass-centric: `receiver_eligibility`, `action_order`, `direction_class`. So the representation does have the
  concept of a game *action* — but only ever as the subject of a legality rule, never as an occurrence.
- **`primaryEvent.conditions[]`** already admits a condition typed **`control`**.

**No row anywhere represents an occurrence of a player contacting the ball.** And one structural fact decides
much of what follows: **there is no individual performer in the representation at all.** Every performers row
is a collection, a count, a role list, a placement group or a participation state. There is no player to be the
subject of a touch.


(b) DOES AN EXISTING TRIGGER OR EVENT CONCEPT OWN IT?

**No. Every trigger that needs a contact consumes it from outside the representation.**

`triggerSemantics` has **exactly one entry** in the whole register, and it is `FIRST_FORWARD_PASS`. Read what
it actually defines: that it is an in-play event rather than a restart; what "forward" is measured against;
what "first" is counted against; and a list of what it does not imply. **It defines the two adverbs and leaves
the verb undefined.** It never says what a pass is.

Two things worth your attention about that entry, both verified by running it:

- It ends *"bounded addition only, no qualifier capability is implied"* — so adding a trigger token does **not**
  give it qualifiers. `triggerQualifiers` covers only two of the nine triggers.
- **Nothing implements the rule the gloss states.** It says that where attacking direction is free the trigger
  is NOT EVALUABLE and its line is CONDITIONAL. `triggerSemantics` is read at exactly two places in the source,
  both in the fidelity layer, neither a per-trigger lookup. The information gate consults the trigger *list*
  and never the semantics. So `FIRST_FORWARD_PASS` is admitted by list membership alone. I am flagging that as a
  gap rather than touching it.

Of the nine triggers, only `POSSESSION_CHANGE` and `FIRST_FORWARD_PASS` presuppose a player-ball interaction,
and neither is defined. The four crossing triggers presuppose ball *motion*, not contact. And
`POSSESSION_CHANGE` is not evaluable: nothing in the corpus says what causes one, the possession row forbids
inventing a mechanism, and in A04 the game carries a possession-change transition over a possession state that
does not exist.


(c) WHAT DOES `LAST_TOUCH` PROVE?

Exactly what you suspected: it is **evidence about an existing semantic concept and is not sufficient as the
trigger.** Its gloss is four words — *"team that touched the ball last"*. `NOT_LAST_TOUCH` has no gloss at all.

**What it proves.** It is a referring expression whose referent is fixed by a predicate over the past, so for it
to denote, three things must already be true of the world the representation describes: contacts occur; they
are attributable to a team; and they are ordered in time, because "last" is a maximum over that order. So the
representation **already assumes a team-attributed, temporally ordered contact history exists.** This is the
same shape of argument as the possession relation — the designation vocabulary named the states of a relation
before anything could hold one.

**What it cannot do.** It cannot identify which player, because its codomain is a team and no player exists. It
cannot say when, beyond "at the trigger it is attached to". It cannot say where. And the applicability rule
restricts it to triggers that put the ball out of play.

**And it is unexercised.** Across the whole corpus, the last-touch qualifier row carries **zero items**, against
**twenty declarations of its absence** — "never examined", "no last-touch qualifier is authored". The one
authored touch-derived value anywhere is GF2's *"restarts from the team that didn't touch it last"*, which is
SUPPORTING and PREFERRED_DEFAULT, so it bounds and never entails — and no goal instantiates an out-of-play
transition at all, so it reaches no consumer.

One thing I should put in front of you because it is yours. **Eight of A01-02's fifteen items are scoped by a
last-touch attribution in their selector**, and each carries your own 26 September definition: a goal kick is
*"the state in which the ball leaves play over the defending team's goal line, having last been touched by an
attacking player, without a goal being scored."* That is a **player-level** touch, from you. The same object
declares that the qualifier it needs is NOT_AUTHORED. So that contract selects elements by a property no
contract establishes.


(d) THE SMALLEST REPRESENTATION — and it is smaller than a touch event

Decompose the condition: *a touch* · *within the channel* · *after which the touching player's team* ·
*retains possession*.

**The fourth part needs nothing new.** `POSSESSION_CHANGE` is the only thing that moves the possession
relation, so "retains" is "no possession change between the touch and the moment".

**The third part needs no player.** The condition's content only ever uses the touching player's *team*. So a
**team-attributed** contact is sufficient here, and no individual performer is required. I want to be careful
about this one, because I got the mirror-image wrong on team equality a day ago: the wording reaches for an
individual, and the claim does not need one. Your goal-kick definition does name a player, so other knowledge
may want more — but this condition does not.

**And the first two parts are mostly already shaped.** This is the encouraging part:

- `valueModifiers[].condition.type` is a closed list reading **`region, object, event`** — it **already admits
  an event-typed condition**.
- `condition.referents` already reads *"references to regions, objects or events"*.
- A **transition** is already the representation's in-play event occurrence, and it already carries a
  **team-designation qualifier** (the last-touch shape) and a **region qualifier**, plus a play state and an
  episode boundary.

So the shape the condition needs exists three times over. What is genuinely missing is narrow: **a trigger
token denoting a contact, a `triggerSemantics` entry defining it, and a `triggerQualifiers` entry giving it the
team and region qualifiers** — that last one explicitly required, because the `FIRST_FORWARD_PASS` precedent
says a bounded trigger addition implies no qualifier capability.

**Two blockers that are yours, not mine.**

First, the specification says, of the primary event's conditions:

    "Each condition refers to existing regions, objects or placements, and is attached to the primary event.
    No condition refers to a player's position during play."

"A touch within the channel" is a claim about where a player-ball contact happened during play. Whether a
**team**-attributed contact located in a region falls inside or outside that commitment is a reading of your
own boundary, and I will not decide it. The two existing in-play condition terms on that side of the line —
"long kick" and "controlled on arrival" — are both FREE under SD-15 precisely because they are in-play
judgements.

Second, `condition.type` admits `event` and `condition.referents` admits event references, but **the
representation holds no event element for them to reference.** There is no events collection; the only
event-shaped things are transitions and the primary event. So either a transition is what an event-typed
condition refers to — which overloads a concept currently about play stopping, continuing and possession
changing — or something new holds it. That is a representational choice with more than one valid reading, so
by your own rule I am bringing it rather than taking it.


(e) WHO ELSE WOULD CONSUME IT

**It is independently required, and the strongest case is your own ruling rather than any drafter's.**

**The Pass Combination Gate.** On 29 September you ruled its reveal trigger: *"The current connected-pass count
is revealed immediately following each qualifying pass and updates progressively as the count changes."* That
became an item with basis OWNER_RULING, REQUIRED, REQUIRED_RANGE, valued `TEAMMATE_ACTION` — and the gap it
replaced recorded the choice explicitly as *"a completed pass (TEAMMATE_ACTION) or the coach's call
(EXTERNAL_SIGNAL)"*. **You chose the pass.** So an entailing owner ruling already depends on a player-ball act
being identifiable.

The rest of that object is the clearest statement of the gap I have seen in the corpus. It authors a pass count,
a reset rule and a chain — and hands the touch-dependent half to play in its own words: *"Whether the passes
were made is play"*, *"The running count is the state of a game in progress"*, *"No field holds reset triggers
or the counting team"*. In a derived game the whole mechanism is two blocks of free text plus an information
rule **whose trigger comes back failed**. There is no count field, no sequence, no event.

**A01-02**, as above — eight items scoped by a last-touch attribution, with your player-level definition behind
them.

**And the counter-precedent, which I think you should weigh against all of it.** Twice now a pass-adjacent
authored need has been met with a *bounded, non-event* addition rather than a contact: `FIRST_FORWARD_PASS` as a
trigger token fenced from implying success, receiver, distance or intent; and the reveal trigger as a
classification of an act rather than the act. Both times the smallest thing that answered the need was not an
event. That is the strongest argument that the answer here might also be smaller than it looks — and it is why I
have not implemented.

Everything else fails the strict test. GF2's last-touch default bounds and never entails. GF4's regain/shot
ordering is SUPPORTING and its own clause says the order and the shot are not held. RPC-001's "controlled on
arrival" is entailing on its field and the specification has already declared it FREE. The Neutral Player
Condition's two positive items are outside the boundary by their own fitNotes.

**The minimum-of-one substitution removed.** Blast radius traced first, as you asked: **one claim corpus-wide,

The distinction that makes it safe: a null minimum is not by itself unreadable. An authored ceiling with no
floor is a complete claim and still works; only a count requirement that stated nothing readable fails. Both
directions are tested, because failing on the wrong one would have been a new defect.
