3 · OBJECT EXISTENCE vs FIXED PLACEMENT — your answer is (b), and it is better than that

**A current invariant incorrectly prevents your distinction.** Not a gap in the object model, and nothing new
is needed.

**First, and this is the sharpest part: the rule that should have handled this is yours, and one register key
routes around it.** A01-02 authors a ball's existence and places no item on either position row. Its
declaration there is `UNDECLARED`, with this note:

    "The ball's layout position was never examined. Its position at the restart moved to T4 (07.a)."

I want to be careful about what that is and is not, because I first read it too generously. `UNDECLARED` does
**not** mean "deliberately unconstrained" — the vocabulary has a separate code for that, `NON_CLAIMED`, and the
very same two rows use it for other object kinds. `UNDECLARED` means **nobody looked**. So this is not an
authored statement that a ball needs no layout position; it is an acknowledged gap, with a note explaining
where the restater thought placement actually belonged — T4, `transitions[].placement.region`, the
placement-by-restart row, which is where your own "necessary only where a restart establishes one" would live.

**And that makes the defect worse rather than milder, because AM-04 is exactly about this case.** Your ruling,
as the engine records it: *"Unexamined silence cannot license a free choice. An `UNDECLARED` declaration
reaching the row bars openness."* That is precisely the situation — and it does not bar openness here, because
`outerBound` satisfies the authority test that AM-04's veto checks. **Your rule is right, it is implemented,
and these two rows are routed past it.**

**Second: the representation already draws your distinction, on separate rows, and a restater has already moved
an item across that boundary for your reason.** `A01-02-07.a`'s own note:

    "The original row was Objects[].position. O4/O5 hold layout, not placement at the moment of a restart,
     so the item moves to T4."

So "layout" and "placement established by a restart" are already distinct rows, and the corpus already treats
them as distinct. What it does **not** have is any authority on the placement row: across the whole corpus T4
carries three items and not one of them can establish anything — two are engine-only and the third is assumed.
**So nothing in the corpus establishes a position by a placement rule either.** Your distinction is recognised
by the structure and empty of authority, which is a different and more tractable problem than a missing concept.

**What overrides it. The requiredness is structural — no item asks for it anywhere.** Three things compose:

1. **Fan-out.** Enumeration creates a line for *every* field row owned by the class's row, so an object gets
   kind, count, along and across automatically. The `applicability` block is the only brake, and it has no
   object entry.
2. **`outerBound: SESSION_ENVELOPE` on those two rows, doing double duty.** It defeats the silence veto that
   would otherwise stop "never examined" becoming a choice; and separately it is the only reason a row whose
   bounds are "inside authored bounds" with *no authored bound* becomes an open choice at all. Remove that one
   key and neither route fires.
3. **Closure.** Realization then refuses to leave any open choice unclosed, with **no exemptions of any kind**.

Worth being exact, because it bears on where a fix belongs: **nothing in any contract item requires a ball's
position.** `requirement` is the predicate kind and `strictness` is evidentiary force; neither can say a field
must be filled. The requiredness is produced entirely by row-level keys and the closure invariant.

**And the invariant is not blanket — it bites precisely on the case you are asking about.** Where a contract
*does* author a position, the line resolves as derived knowledge and never becomes a choice at all: in my
earlier experiment the flipped GF2 target came back with its along-position **entailed** from GF2-06.b, and the
refusal named only its kind and its across-position. So objects whose placement someone authored are fine. What
gets forced is exactly an object whose location nobody authored because its location is state-dependent — a
ball. The invariant singles out the dynamic case, which is the opposite of what you would want it to do.

**The decisive contrast, and it is one key wide.** Only four rows in the whole register carry `outerBound`:
the two space rows, and object position along and across. **Object *count* carries none** — so under
*identical* silence, the same ball's count lands `NOT_AUTHORED`, an honest returned gap, while its position is
forced into a choice. Same object, same silence, opposite outcome, and the only difference is that one key.

**And the compelled choice is not even checked.** Its bounds are empty and its permitted set is null, so the
bound check returns `UNBOUNDED`. The invariant is not protecting a spatial constraint here; it is compelling an
uncheckable invention.

**The mechanism you need already exists, is general, and is already in production.** The part that decides a
line's state is plain register data: the enumerator and the stage-6 classifier both read the `applicability`
entry generically and name **no row id at all**, and it already conditions on an element's own selector
attribute for the transition qualifiers. That is precisely the half your question turns on.

One honest qualification, because I nearly overstated this. RC-20's *semantics* **are** separately hardcoded —
the transition-coherence gate reads T6/T3/T4/T5 with the literals CONTINUE and STOP_RESUME directly, without
consulting `applicability`. So "nothing is special-cased" would be wrong. What is generic is the line-state
machinery, not every consequence drawn from it; and the practical implication for you is that an entry on the
position rows would withdraw the lines correctly, while **no gate would then assert anything about object
placement** — which is the right outcome here, but it should be a decision rather than a surprise.
For an object that attribute is `kind`, which is exactly what distinguishes a ball from a goal.

**I proved the counterfactual rather than arguing it.** With an `applicability` entry on the two position rows
— **register data only, no code change** — a ball's position lines become WITHDRAWN, leave the open list, are
**not** reported as a gap either, the ball remains fully present in the game as `{kind: "ball"}`, and the
position refusals disappear. That is your third state, and it is already running for transition qualifiers.

**One hazard before you authorize anything.** Where an element's `kind` is *not* fixed by its selector, the
line goes CONDITIONAL instead of WITHDRAWN, and a conditional line appears in **none** of the resolved game's
three lists — so it would vanish silently rather than be reported. Two corpus objects use an open selector. I
would want that closed, or explicitly accepted, before an entry goes in.

Nothing authored, nothing in the object model altered, no position written, as you instructed.
